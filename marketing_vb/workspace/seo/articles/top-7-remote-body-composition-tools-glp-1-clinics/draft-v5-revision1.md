# 7 Body Composition and Progress-Tracking Tools for Remote GLP-1 Clinics

## Why Tool Choice Is Its Own Problem for GLP-1 Clinics

A GLP-1 clinic rarely needs one tool to perform every measurement task. It needs a combination that fits remote delivery, is practical for patients to use repeatedly, and produces data the care team can review consistently.

Most GLP-1 programs run longitudinal, with members enrolled for 30, 60, or 90 days and often longer, and repeat check-ins that happen mostly from home. Measurement has to travel to the patient, not the patient to the measurement. A device that lives in a clinic room measures precisely and reaches almost no one between appointments.

Scale weight alone hides what matters during weight change during GLP-1-supported programs. Two members can drop the same number of kilograms while one preserves lean mass and the other loses it. The scale reports one figure and says nothing about that split. Body composition is what separates the two stories.

Not every category evaluated here is remote, and not every category is a precise body-composition method. Professional bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) devices, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and manual clinic measurement all require an in-person visit. Progress photos and most wearables provide engagement or activity data rather than structured composition measurement. All seven still belong in this evaluation, because remote GLP-1 programs use them together as complementary parts of a tracking workflow, not as seven substitutes competing for the same job. For the market context behind that framing, see our [GLP-1 Market hub](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/glp-1-market/).

## Short Answer: Which Tools Should a GLP-1 Clinic Evaluate?

Seven tool categories make up the practical landscape a GLP-1 clinic will evaluate. They are not ranked. No scoring methodology in this piece establishes that one category outperforms another across every use, and the right mix depends on the program's protocol, population, and cadence.

- Connected scales (smart scales with at-home BIA)
- Professional BIA devices (clinic-grade)
- DXA access services
- Mobile body scanning from photos
- Manual measurement (tape, calipers, in-clinic)
- Patient-uploaded progress photos
- Wearables with body-composition estimates

*Scope note: what follows is a tools-evaluation resource for GLP-1 clinics and virtual weight-loss teams. It is not medical advice, and none of these tools should be treated as a way to diagnose a condition or to make eligibility, prescribing, or treatment decisions. Those decisions stay with clinicians and program protocols.*

For the underlying "what is body composition and how is it measured" definition, see [how to measure body composition](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/how-to-measure-body-composition/). For why Body Mass Index (BMI) alone tells a partial story, see [beyond BMI](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/beyond-bmi-business/).

## Why Remote Body Composition Tracking Matters Now

GLP-1 delivery has grown into a largely remote, telehealth-first category. J.P. Morgan Research forecasts the global incretin market, which includes GLP-1s, to reach [about $200 billion by 2030](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/global-research/current-events/obesity-drugs), with the number of Americans on GLP-1 treatment rising from roughly 10 million in 2025 toward 25 million by 2030. Coverage is expanding too: the [KFF 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2025-employer-health-benefits-survey/) found that 43 percent of firms with 5,000 or more workers covered GLP-1 drugs for weight loss in 2025, up from 28 percent in 2024.

Composition, not just weight, is the clinical detail that matters most against that backdrop. Preserving lean mass during weight change during GLP-1-supported programs is a recognized concern: Mass General Brigham clinicians describe lean body mass loss as a real consideration during rapid weight reduction, note that it is not unique to GLP-1 therapy, and state that [more accurate tools for measuring body composition are needed](https://advances.massgeneral.org/endocrinology/article.aspx?id=1601) alongside periodic monitoring. A remote-first patient population combined with a composition question that scale weight cannot answer is why tool selection for tracking is now a deliberate operational decision.

## The Seven Categories

### Connected Scales (Smart Scales with At-Home BIA)

Connected scales combine at-home weight tracking with a single-frequency BIA composition estimate in one step. Example service models include consumer connected-scale product lines, provided directly to members or supported as a bring-your-own-device option.

- **Data captured:** weight, plus a single-frequency composition estimate (typically body fat percentage)
- **Where capture occurs:** at home, unsupervised
- **Hardware required:** a connected scale (member-owned or clinic-issued)
- **Integration options:** most consumer models sync through a manufacturer's companion app; enterprise or API-level data access varies by manufacturer and should be confirmed directly with the vendor
- **Main source of variability:** home BIA estimates can vary with hydration, food intake, exercise, and measurement timing. Standardized conditions are important when comparing readings over time.
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** high-frequency weight trend with a light, directional composition signal, not the record a program should lean on for milestone composition claims
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** What frequency does the device use? Is data exportable through an API? What conditions does the manufacturer recommend for consistent readings?

For depth on where connected scales fit, see [body composition scale](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/body-composition-scale/).

### Professional BIA Devices (Clinic-Grade)

Clinic-grade BIA systems support structured in-clinic composition checkpoints. Example service models are the multi-frequency, segmental analyzers used in clinic settings, the category referenced in comparisons such as InBody-class devices.

- **Data captured:** segmental composition (arm, leg, trunk), body fat percentage, lean mass; specifics vary by device
- **Where capture occurs:** in-clinic, supervised
- **Hardware required:** a clinic-grade BIA analyzer
- **Integration options:** device-dependent; many clinic systems export through proprietary software rather than an open API
- **Main source of variability:** hydration and physiological state at the time of the reading, the same sensitivity home BIA has. Professional BIA systems may provide multi-frequency and segmental estimates, depending on the device.
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** a structured in-clinic checkpoint for members who are physically present, not a remote monitoring layer
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** Is the device single- or multi-frequency? What validation population was it tested against? Can results export in a structured, comparable format?

For method depth, see [BIA scanning](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/bia-scan/) and the [InBody vs 3DLOOK comparison](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/inbody-vs-3dlook-the-future-of-body-composition-measurement/).

### DXA Access Services

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) provides detailed estimates of fat mass, lean mass, and bone mineral content and is commonly used as a reference method in body-composition assessment. Access services route members to an imaging center or hospital-based facility for a scan.

- **Data captured:** fat mass, lean mass, bone mineral content, regional breakdowns
- **Where capture occurs:** an imaging center or hospital, appointment-based
- **Hardware required:** none for the clinic; the imaging center owns the equipment
- **Integration options:** results typically arrive as a report (PDF or portal export) rather than a live data feed; confirm the delivery format with the imaging provider
- **Main source of variability:** device calibration and scan protocol at the imaging site; comparability across different facilities or device models should not be assumed
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** periodic detailed assessment when required by the program, not a frequent monitoring tool
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** What is the appointment lead time and geographic coverage? How is the report delivered, and can it be structured for the patient record? What is the cost per scan at program volume?

For the full comparison of DXA and mobile methods, see [AI body scanners vs DXA scans](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/ai-body-scanners-vs-dexa-scans/).

### Mobile Body Scanning from Photos (FitXpress)

Mobile body scanning turns two smartphone photos, front and side, into structured measurements, composition outputs, and a 3D model, with no dedicated hardware. In the FitXpress implementation, the full pipeline runs in under 45 seconds and returns 80+ body measurements per scan, alongside composition outputs including Body Mass Index (BMI), basal metabolic rate (BMR), body fat percentage, lean mass, fat mass, essential fat, and beneficial fat.

- **Data captured:** 80+ body measurements, BMI, BMR, body fat percentage, lean mass, fat mass, essential fat, beneficial fat, and a 3D model
- **Where capture occurs:** anywhere with a smartphone; no clinic visit required
- **Hardware required:** a smartphone with a camera; no dedicated scanning hardware
- **Integration options:** white-label delivery via application programming interface (API) and software development kit (SDK)
- **Main source of variability:** capture conditions (distance, lighting, pose); a guided capture flow and retake logic reduce, but do not remove, this variability
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** remote intake and frequent between-visit check-ins that produce a structured record for clinician review, alongside a reference method at milestones
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** What validation population and reference method were used? How repeatable are results under normal at-home conditions? What happens to photos after processing?

For most evaluated measurements, repeated scans showed typical scan-to-scan differences of less than 1 cm. This repeatability supports more consistent longitudinal comparison when scans are completed under similar capture conditions. FitXpress is intended to show meaningful progress over time rather than very small short-term changes. Separately, against expert manual measurement, FitXpress reports 96-97% accuracy with a typical error margin of 1.5-2.0 cm. Those are two distinct benchmarks against two different references and should be read separately; the full [mobile body scanning accuracy](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/mobile-body-scanning-accuracy/) framework covers how to read them by decision and reference.

FitXpress's predicted-weight output showed a mean absolute error of approximately 3.5% in the evaluated dataset under the specified capture conditions. This is a software estimate, not a physical scale reading. For how hardware scanners and mobile capture differ, see [body scanner machines vs mobile 3D body scan](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/body-scanner-machines-vs-mobile-3d-body-scan/).

### Manual Measurement (Tape, Calipers, In-Clinic)

Manual anthropometry uses a trained staff member with a tape measure and calipers during an in-person visit, providing staff-led in-clinic circumference tracking.

- **Data captured:** circumferences and skinfold measurements that feed a composition estimate
- **Where capture occurs:** in-clinic, staff-administered
- **Hardware required:** tape measure and calipers; minimal device budget
- **Integration options:** typically manual entry into the patient record; no native structured export
- **Main source of variability:** the measuring staff member; readings shift between different staff and between sessions with the same member
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** an occasional in-clinic capture where a clinician is already present, supplementing remote data rather than replacing it
- **Questions to ask (internal protocol):** Is there a standardized measurement protocol across staff? How is inter-rater consistency checked?

### Patient-Uploaded Progress Photos

Self-captured progress photos cost nothing and engage members emotionally, which is why many programs already collect them.

- **Data captured:** unstructured images; no numeric measurement
- **Where capture occurs:** at home, member-captured
- **Hardware required:** a smartphone camera
- **Integration options:** typically stored in a patient app or portal as an image, not a structured data field
- **Main source of variability:** patient photos usually provide qualitative rather than structured measurement data. Differences in pose, distance, clothing, lighting, and camera angle can limit direct comparison.
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** engagement and motivation, layered on top of a structured measurement source rather than standing in for one
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** Is there guidance for consistent pose, distance, and lighting? Is the photo stored alongside structured measurement data from the same session?

For turning visual progress into something that supports adherence and retention, see [visual progress tracking for GLP-1 adherence and retention](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/visual-progress-tracking-glp1-adherence-retention/).

### Wearables with Body-Composition Estimates

Some wearables provide device-specific body-composition estimates, often through an on-demand impedance measurement. The wider category is more established for activity, sleep, heart-rate, and recovery data than for body composition.

- **Data captured:** activity, sleep, heart rate, and recovery data as the core function; body-composition estimates only on select devices, usually through an on-demand reading
- **Where capture occurs:** worn at home or through the day
- **Hardware required:** a wearable device (wrist-worn or similar)
- **Integration options:** most consumer wearables sync through a companion app; API access to composition-specific data varies widely by manufacturer
- **Main source of variability:** device-dependent; the estimation method and its accuracy vary significantly across manufacturers and models
- **Best role in a GLP-1 workflow:** passive engagement and trend-spotting between check-ins, complementing a structured capture method rather than replacing it
- **Questions to ask the vendor:** Does the device estimate body composition at all, or only activity and recovery metrics? If so, on what basis, and how was it validated?

## How to Build the Right Tool Mix

A hybrid model may suit programs that require periodic in-person assessment while also needing more frequent remote progress data. The appropriate combination depends on the program's clinical protocol, patient population, cadence, and operational requirements, not a single formula that applies to every clinic.

In practice, that often means pairing one category built for periodic, higher-fidelity assessment, such as DXA or professional BIA, with one category built for frequent, low-burden remote capture, such as connected scales or mobile body scanning, and layering an engagement tool such as progress photos or a wearable on top where it supports retention. For the compliance dimension specific to GLP-1 delivery, see the [GLP-1 compliance challenge](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/glp-1-compliance-challenge/), and for regulated BMI-verification workflows, the [online pharmacy BMI verification guide](https://3dlook.ai/content-hub/online-pharmacy-bmi-verification-a-2026-compliance-guide/).

## Buyer Checklist

Score every candidate tool against the same questions, regardless of category:

1. What output does the tool measure directly, and what does it estimate?
2. What reference method and population were used for validation?
3. How repeatable are results under normal at-home conditions?
4. What instructions and capture-quality checks are included?
5. What hardware must the clinic or patient purchase?
6. Can results be exported through an API or SDK?
7. How are photos, measurements, identifiers, and session data retained?
8. Can the tool support the intended patient volume and check-in cadence?
9. Where is clinician review required?
10. Which program decisions should the output explicitly not support?

## Implementation and Evaluation Considerations

Capture conditions shape the quality of any remote measurement, so rollout details matter as much as the tool itself. A guided capture flow, retake logic, and consistent conditions across sessions reduce variability, though clear member instructions and deployment-specific thresholds still do real work on top.

Cadence should start with a baseline at intake, then recur on a schedule aligned to program check-ins. Run a time-boxed pilot before committing at scale, and measure capture completion rate, record consistency across sessions, and clinician review time; those numbers show whether a tool fits the workflow better than a spec sheet can. API and SDK delivery allow customers to connect outputs with their existing applications and data workflows, so structured data reaches the systems the care team already uses.

There is no single best tool; there are seven categories that fit different decisions. Connected scales, professional BIA, DXA access, mobile body scanning, manual clinic measurement, patient photos, and wearables each trade remote capability, burden, and cost differently. A hybrid model may suit programs that require periodic in-person assessment while also needing more frequent remote progress data.

**Can a smartphone body scan replace DXA or professional BIA?**
Mobile body scanning serves a different role. It supports lower-burden remote measurements and longitudinal comparison, while DXA, professional BIA, calibrated scales, and other methods remain appropriate wherever required by the program's protocol.

**What body-composition data do these tools capture?**
Depending on the category, they capture fat mass, lean mass, body fat percentage, and circumferences. FitXpress specifically outputs BMI, BMR, body fat percentage, lean mass, fat mass, essential fat, and beneficial fat, alongside 80+ measurements per scan.

**How often should a GLP-1 program track body composition?**
A common pattern is a baseline at intake followed by recurring check-ins aligned to the program's schedule. The exact cadence is a clinical and protocol decision for each program, not something a tool should dictate.

**Is remote body composition data used to make eligibility or treatment decisions?**
No. This data is supporting, structured evidence for review. Clinicians and program protocols make eligibility and treatment decisions, and the tools standardize the capture that informs them.

**Is FitXpress positioned as a medical device?**
It is not positioned as a medical device.

**Which tool has the lowest patient burden?**
Connected scales, wearables, and two-photo mobile scanning are the lowest-friction options, since they capture in seconds without a visit. Reference methods like DXA carry higher burden because they require an appointment.

**How accurate is mobile body scanning for GLP-1 tracking?**
The useful question is "accurate for which decision, against which reference?" For most evaluated measurements, repeated scans showed typical scan-to-scan differences of less than 1 cm. This repeatability supports more consistent longitudinal comparison when scans are completed under similar capture conditions, and FitXpress is intended to show meaningful progress over time rather than very small short-term changes. Accuracy against expert manual measurement is a separate benchmark: 96-97%, with a typical error margin of 1.5-2.0 cm.
