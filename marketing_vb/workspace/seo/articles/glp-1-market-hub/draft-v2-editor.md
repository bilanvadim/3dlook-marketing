# GLP-1 Market Growth and the Need for Better Patient Progress Tracking

## The GLP-1 Market Moment

Few categories in healthcare have scaled as fast as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) medications for weight management. Prescribing volumes have climbed sharply, and a wave of dedicated weight-loss programs has grown up around the drugs themselves. GLP-1 market growth started inside diabetes care and has widened into a broad weight-management market with its own operators and economics, and a retention problem to match.

That growth pulled in far more than drug makers. Telehealth clinics, in-person and hybrid weight-loss clinics, pharmacy-led programs, and employer and wellness platforms have all built GLP-1 offerings. Each runs a longitudinal program: a patient starts on a medication, then checks in over weeks and months while their body changes, and the commercial model depends on those repeat check-ins continuing.

Here is the operator tension. Onboarding a GLP-1 patient is comparatively easy. Keeping that patient engaged for 30, 60, and 90 days is hard, and it gets harder when progress is not visible between appointments. A patient who sees no change beyond a single number on a scale is a patient at risk of quiet drop-off, and programs that cannot show progress lose the members whose retention pays for the program.

This hub covers both halves of that story: the scale and speed of GLP-1 weight-loss programs growth, and the progress-tracking gap that growth has exposed. The sections that follow map the market, name the gap, and show where structured body-data capture fits for program operators.

## GLP-1 Market Growth: Size and Trajectory

The headline figures are large and rising. J.P. Morgan Research projects the global market for GLP-1 and related incretin therapies to reach roughly **$200 billion by 2030**, framing obesity treatment as one of the fastest-growing areas in pharmaceuticals ([J.P. Morgan Research](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/global-research/current-events/obesity-drugs)). The trajectory is driven by volume rather than price, as more patients start and stay on treatment.

Patient counts show the same climb. The same analysis estimates that GLP-1 use in the United States rose from about 6 million people in 2024 to roughly 10 million in 2025, and projects 25 to 30 million Americans on these medications by 2030. That is a large, growing population moving through weight-management programs that need to document and review progress over time.

The runway extends further still. Only about 7% of people with diabetes and roughly 2% of the eligible obese population globally were using GLP-1 medications, which leaves substantial headroom as the use case widens from diabetes management into dedicated weight management. GLP-1 market size is expanding on that shift.

Employer behavior shows the same widening. The Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey found that **43% of firms with 5,000 or more workers covered GLP-1 agonists when used primarily for weight loss in 2025, up from 28% the year before** ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2025-employer-health-benefits-survey/)). Coverage of the weight-loss indication, distinct from diabetes, is moving into mainstream employer benefits.

Digital-health entrants show how fast programs are scaling around the medication. Hims & Hers reported $2.35 billion in total revenue for 2025, up from $1.5 billion in 2024, ended the year with 2.5 million subscribers, and projected $2.7 to $2.9 billion in 2026, with its weight-management offering a named growth driver ([STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2026/02/23/him-hers-earnings-2026-outlook-compounded-semaglutide/)). Telehealth GLP-1 programs are not a niche experiment but a scaled, competitive market, and every one of them has to keep members engaged across a longitudinal program.

## The Progress-Tracking Gap

Most GLP-1 programs track progress with a thin set of signals: scale weight, patient self-report, and the occasional manually captured progress photo. That combination was inherited from the pre-medication weight-loss world, and it holds up poorly under the demands of a longitudinal GLP-1 program.

The first problem is composition. A scale returns one number, and that number cannot separate fat loss from lean-mass change. During rapid GLP-1 weight change, that distinction matters: two patients can lose the same weight while their body composition moves in opposite directions, and scale weight hides the difference entirely.

The second problem is consistency. Self-reported measurements are easy to misstate, and manual progress photos are captured under different lighting, poses, and phone angles each time. There is no reliable way to line up one month against the next when the inputs keep shifting. The visual record exists, but it is not comparable.

The third problem is what this does to patients. Progress that is invisible feels like progress that is not happening. When the scale stalls for a week during an otherwise healthy change in body composition, a patient with no richer signal often reads that as failure, and that is the moment programs lose people, usually between appointments where no one is watching.

The gap also weakens clinical review. A care team that opens a patient record and finds a single weight value and a free-text note has little structured data to work from and little that is easy to verify. Thin, hard-to-compare inputs make review slower and less confident. Structured, repeatable body measurement is the foundation of better GLP-1 patient progress tracking, and the sections below get specific about what that involves.

## Why Progress Tracking Is Becoming Table Stakes

Visible, measurable progress is shifting from a differentiator to an expectation. As GLP-1 programs mature and compete, the ones that keep members are the ones that can show a member what is changing, and the ones that keep payer and employer partners are the ones that can document outcomes.

Retention pressure is the first driver. GLP-1 program economics depend on members staying through the months where results compound, and a repeat check-in only sustains retention when it shows the member something worth returning for. A number on a scale rarely carries that engagement.

Review quality is the second driver. Clinicians working through dozens or hundreds of patients need structured data that is fast to scan and consistent from visit to visit. As caseloads grow, the quality of the underlying record becomes the constraint on how well a team can review at scale.

Accountability to partners is the third driver. Employers and health plans that now cover the weight-loss indication increasingly expect programs to report measurable, defensible outcomes, and body composition tracking for GLP-1 programs is becoming part of that expectation. Self-report alone rarely meets it.

## Body Composition Beyond the Scale

Scale weight and BMI are partial signals. Both compress a complex physical change into one figure, and neither can tell a program whether a drop in weight came from fat, from lean mass, or from fluid. For a GLP-1 program that limitation sits at the center of the tracking problem, because preserving lean mass during rapid weight loss is a recognized clinical concern.

Body composition is a richer longitudinal signal. Fat percentage, lean mass, and fat mass, tracked over time, describe the shape of a change rather than only its magnitude. A program that can see composition can tell a healthy trajectory from a concerning one, and it can show a member real progress even in a week when the scale barely moves. That matters most during rapid GLP-1 weight change, when the pace of loss raises the stakes on what is being lost.
