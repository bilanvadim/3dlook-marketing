Most fitness apps personalise to a demographic profile, not an individual body.

Age, weight, stated goals — that is the typical input set. It produces a plan fitted to a category, not a person. Two users with the same age and goal but different body shapes get the same programme.

Structured body data changes the input layer. Body shape and proportions can inform how an exercise is cued or modified. Someone with different limb-to-torso proportions may need a different setup for the same movement. Body-composition trends over time give a second signal beyond weight — a change in estimated lean mass may prompt a coach to review nutrition, training load, and recovery before adjusting.

This works alongside other inputs — goals, mobility, fitness level, performance — rather than replacing them. Measurements provide additional context, not a standalone basis for prescribing exercises.

From a product perspective, this is the difference between a generic plan and one that adapts to how a specific body responds. It is the same direction personalisation has moved in every consumer category — from demographic targeting to individual adaptation.
