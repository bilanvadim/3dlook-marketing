## instagram-company

The first body measurement happens before anyone walks into the clinic.

A bariatric program sends a scan link. At home, the patient follows guided prompts and takes two photos, front and side, on their own phone. No specialized hardware. Results typically return in under 45 seconds.

By the time the consultation starts, the numbers are already in the record. The appointment can be about medical history, comorbidities, surgical risk and patient education. The measuring already happened.

A single record may hold a capture timestamp, selected body measurements, two BMI values for comparison, and any quality flags. What it never holds is a decision. FitXpress does not diagnose conditions, make clinical decisions, or determine treatment eligibility. Clinical teams and payers keep those.

## facebook-company

Bariatric care does not end at the procedure, and neither does the measuring. A scan taken before surgery can become the baseline for every scan that follows, as long as the later ones use the same guided sequence and return the same fields.

That matters more than it used to. Johns Hopkins researchers, reporting on a JAMA Surgery analysis, found that roughly one in seven bariatric surgery patients used new weight-loss drugs after surgery. Follow-up is rarely one straight line any more.

Weight, circumference measurements and body composition estimates each describe a different part of postoperative change. When the care team deems them appropriate, these values can be reviewed alongside medication history.

The record itself stays modest. It is a dated series of selected metrics. The program sets the cadence and decides which fields to keep.

Here is the question we keep coming back to. If a patient's body-data record started at the first intake instead of the surgical consultation, how much more of their trajectory would your team see?

## linkedin-company

Obesity care has a sequencing problem, and it rarely gets named.

The most recent clinical measurement cycle from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), conducted from August 2021 to August 2023, found that 40.3% of US adults had obesity and 9.4% had severe obesity. The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery states that less than 1% of eligible patients undergo surgery in a given year. A 2026 narrative review identified preoperative attrition reported as high as 60%, while other cohorts in that literature reported substantially different rates.

Earlier body data does not resolve those gaps. What it changes is where a program can measure.

Bariatric programs need body metrics at two points: before clinical review begins, and during follow-up after treatment. When the first usable record appears only at the consultation, staff may need additional outreach before they can complete pre-qualification or prepare a prior-authorization packet.

A guided two-photo scan completed before that appointment returns a structured record: a capture timestamp, selected body measurements, two BMI values for comparison, and capture-quality flags. FitXpress supplies those inputs. Clinical eligibility remains with the care team, and each payer decides whether submitted documentation meets its requirements.

The business value sits in what a program can then measure: intake completion, time to a completed record, prior-authorization rework, measurement-only appointments, and follow-up completion.

## linkedin-katerina

A single BMI reading is becoming weaker evidence every year.

Here is why. In May, the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery reported a conference study of more than 6,700 patients. All had used glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) medications before bariatric surgery. On average, these patients lost approximately 8% of total body weight before the procedure.

A patient can therefore arrive at intake with a current BMI below a value documented months earlier. Both numbers are real. Only one of them carries a date and a recorded source.

That is the shift I keep watching. Enterprise health programs are learning to ask for provenance as well as precision.

My own rule of thumb: a body-data field that cannot say when it was captured and by what method is not documentation yet.

## linkedin-vadim

The scans that decide your rollout are the ones that never complete.

Remote capture works well until it reaches a patient who cannot stand unassisted, or who is following the instructions in a second language. That patient still needs a record. If the workflow has no answer, a coordinator improvises one, and records stop being comparable across sites.

Two things I would settle before a pilot starts.

First, write the fallback down. Who books the in-clinic measurement, and at which point in the retake sequence does it trigger?

Second, measure completion twice. Once for patients who finish unaided, once for those who needed staff or caregiver help. A single blended completion rate hides the cost of the second group.

The rest of the pilot is easier than those two.

What share of your remote captures need a second attempt right now, and can you break that number down by site?

## linkedin-nick

The new prior-authorization clock does not cover every bariatric case.

Under CMS-0057-F, the prior-authorization rule from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, new timeframes took effect on January 1, 2026. Medicare Advantage organizations and specified Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program payers must issue standard non-drug decisions within 7 calendar days, and expedited decisions within 72 hours.

Medicare fee-for-service sits outside those provisions. The decision-timeframe provisions also did not change the requirements for Qualified Health Plans on Federally Facilitated Exchanges.

For a bariatric program, the faster clock covers part of your book only. Worth confirming which part before you rebuild intake around it.

The move I would make first: take one plan, map what its packet needs for body-data documentation, then check what you hold on consultation day. The gap is usually a date or a source field.

Which covers more of your bariatric volume today: plans inside CMS-0057-F, or plans outside it?

## linkedin-olena

Most remote-capture pilots settle data governance last. That is the wrong order.

A scan produces two things with different lifespans. Photos can be deleted immediately after processing or within 30 days, depending on the customer's policy. The record built from them, the measurements, the timestamp, the quality flags, is what your workflows keep using after the pilot ends.

Roles have to be explicit before the first capture. In most enterprise deployments, the customer acts as controller and 3DLOOK acts as processor under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Encryption at rest and in transit is the straightforward part. Who may open a record, and for how long, is what programs settle too late.

I put retention, access control and photo deletion in the pilot plan itself, next to capture completion.

When your last pilot ended, who owned the records it produced, and for how long?

## linkedin-katya

Deployments stall on one unanswered question: who acts when the system raises a flag.

Here is the mechanic. At intake, a program can compare BMI from self-reported height and weight with BMI from the same height and the predicted weight from the scan. A difference above the configured threshold is routed for human review.

That is where the commercial conversation usually goes quiet. The software flags the discrepancy. The program decides whether the patient needs a retake, an in-clinic measurement, or further review. If no one owns that step, the flag becomes a ticket nobody closes, and the technology takes the blame.

Before signing anything, I would put one question to the program itself: which role reviews a flagged record, and what is that person allowed to do next?

How long does a flagged record sit in your queue before someone opens it?
