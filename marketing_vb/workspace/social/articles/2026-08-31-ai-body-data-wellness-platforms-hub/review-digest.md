## linkedin-katerina

Accuracy is the wrong first question for longitudinal progress tracking.

Most teams evaluating body-measurement technology ask how accurate it is against a reference method. Fair question. It is just not the one that decides whether a wellness program works.

For tracking change over time, repeatability matters more. If scan-to-scan variation is larger than a member's actual change, the progress view invents movement that isn't there, or misses movement that is. Accuracy won't save you from that.

So I'd reframe the evaluation. Ask for repeatability under comparable conditions, not only accuracy against a reference. They are different claims, and they answer different questions.

What I'm seeing now: the strongest buyers ask for both, separately, and reject figures that blur them.

## linkedin-katya

Scanning members too often is how you kill a good progress feature.

Commercially, the temptation is to scan weekly. More data, more engagement, right? Usually the opposite. If you scan faster than the body actually changes, the comparison shows noise, members stop believing it, and the feature quietly dies.

The practical starting point from the article: for body-change programs, 4-12 weeks is a reasonable range, adjusted to the goal and the expected rate of change. A waist-focused goal moves on a different clock than a broad body-composition one.

So I'd set cadence to the goal, not to the dashboard. Fewer scans that each show real change beat frequent scans that show nothing.

That's what builds trust, and trust is what earns renewals in digital health.

## linkedin-nick

The number that tells you a body-scan program is working is the second-scan rate.

Plenty of programs can get a member to complete one scan. Onboarding energy carries it. The baseline looks great in a demo.

But a single scan is a snapshot. The value shows up only when a member comes back weeks later for a follow-up, and the two can be compared on the same measurements. No second scan, no progress signal, no engagement loop.

So when I look at an early rollout, I don't start with accuracy. I look at completion rate, retake rate, and above all the second-scan rate. Those tell you whether people can finish the flow and whether they return.

For GLP-1 and remote monitoring programs, that return visit is the whole point, since scale weight alone hides fat mass and lean mass changes.

Body scanning in a wellness product raises a fair question from every member: where do my photos go? If the program can't answer clearly, adoption stalls before the first scan.

The part teams often skip is the split of responsibility. In most enterprise deployments, the customer acts as the data controller and 3DLOOK acts as the data processor under GDPR. That isn't a legal footnote. It shapes what you tell members, who can access results, and how long anything is kept.

One concrete detail worth knowing: photos are permanently removed immediately after processing or within 30 days, and temporarily retained photos are automatically blurred.

My rule of thumb: write the privacy explanation before you design the progress view. Trust is what earns the second scan.

## linkedin-vadim

Most bad progress data comes from the capture step, not the algorithm.

Here's the failure mode I'd watch for. You roll out body scanning to thousands of members, on their own phones, in their own bedrooms. Different lighting. Different clothing. Different camera height. The baseline and the follow-up get captured under conditions that don't match.

Now your comparison is measuring the room, not the body.

The fix isn't a better model. It's a guided capture flow: the same pose each time, a pose check before the shot, and a retake that takes seconds. Boring, operational, and it's what makes a two-scan comparison mean anything.

For most evaluated FitXpress measurements, repeated scans showed typical scan-to-scan differences of less than 1 cm. That only holds if the conditions hold. So I'd treat capture guidance as a feature, not an afterthought.
